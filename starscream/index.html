Abstract

We pretrain an agile drone-racing base model entirely from supervised labels, with no reinforcement learning, to isolate what can be learned as representation before any policy-gradient fine-tuning. An MPCC expert labels the states the learner actually reaches. The learner flies coherent two-to-four-gate segments on its own while the expert only labels. A 5.84 M-parameter causal transformer reads the next six gates as ordered route tokens, a settings token describing the randomized vehicle it is flying, and a commanded speed it can be given at test time.

The current base completes 92.2% of 3,200 randomized starts on 100 held-out hard courses and 91.0% of 1,920 starts on 60 championship-style courses. The previous base, reported on this page until now, completed 43.8% and 55.6% of the same panels. The new recipe also learns far faster: it reaches 50% completion on its selection panel in 6.4 M environment steps, where the earlier recipe needed 108.7 M. Most of the remaining gap is quality rather than survival. 68.8% of starts are both clean and on time, recoveries after missed gates are common, and a few public reference courses stay hard. This checkpoint is the privileged teacher for the vision-based student we distill next; student results will be released with the preprint.

Results

One checkpoint, before any reinforcement learning: round 71 of the scaled-D run, at 29,465,874 environment steps. It is evaluated on two independent panels of course geometry it never trained on, each course flown from 32 randomized starts on freshly sampled vehicles.

The hard-panel deficit is gone

The earlier base was much weaker on the hard panel than on the championship-style one: 43.8% against 55.6%. The new base completes both at over 91%, and the hard panel is no longer the weaker of the two.

Completion, pace and clean flight, kept separate

Finishing is only one bar. Timely means the lap also beats a per-course deadline, set at 1.5× the expert reference time or reference + 3 s, whichever is larger. Clean means no gate was missed along the way. The strictest bar requires all three, and 68.8% of real100-v2 starts clear it.

Gains across route families

The gain is not concentrated in a few easy families: 14 of 15 improved, and go-around held at 83%. Long-low courses, the clearest failure of the earlier base, went from 6.3% to 98.8%, and flow courses from 17.2% to 93.4%. Where the families still differ is in quality. Go-around, slalom and ordered-3D courses are finished almost every time but are often not flown clean and on time.

Course by course

Each point is one of the 100 hard courses. Points above the diagonal improved. 99 of 100 courses finish at least as often as before, and 59 now complete all 32 starts. The spread along the bottom-right shows where the earlier base was already strong. The points that stay low are public reference layouts.

Learning, an order of magnitude faster

The comparison is a from-scratch run of the previous recipe with the settings token already added. It shares the same 65 training courses, 25 frozen selection courses, and episodes, updates and batch size per round. Because it already read the vehicle, the speed-up comes from the rest of the recipe.

Completion arrived first and quality followed. In the early rounds almost every completion involved a recovery; by round 13 81% still did. After that, clean and on-time flight kept climbing long after completion had flattened.

How to read the speed-up. These are first crossings from one training run per recipe. The earlier run was evaluated with 4 starts per course and a 10,000-step horizon, scaled D with 8 starts and 6,000 steps. Several changes are bundled together (next section). The result is a recipe-level efficiency gain, not a multiplier that can be assigned to any single change.

What changed from the previous base

Until this update, this page reported the v6.21.1 base. It was trained for 121.3 M environment steps with per-step mixing of expert and learner control. It completed 55.6% of real60 and 43.8% of real100-v2 starts, recovered from missed gates, and was not given the parameters of the vehicle it was flying. The current base changes the recipe in five places at once.

  • Clean training rowscorrectness

    The policy reads its own previous command as part of its state, which matters because of the 11 ms action delay. In the earlier pipeline that field was encoded with a legacy map that saturates at 30 m/s² of collective thrust, while commands run to 40. In replay, 47% of executed commands were above that line. An offline remapping step also overwrote the field in stored samples. As a result, a large share of training rows gave the policy a wrong picture of how hard it was already pushing. The field now follows the configured action contract everywhere. Labels and the 40 m/s² thrust authority are unchanged.

    Three smaller repairs went in with it. Masked auxiliary losses no longer dilute their gradients by the valid-row fraction. Replay quota rounding no longer favors the first families and gates. Shard integrity checks were tightened as a precaution.

  • Learner-flown collectionstate relevance

    Instead of mixing expert and learner actions step by step, the learner now flies coherent segments on its own, ending after two to four accepted gates. The MPCC expert labels every state it reaches but never takes control. Half the episodes start from an expert-flown prefix, so later parts of the course are covered too. The learner sees the errors it will actually make, and bounded segments stop long failed recoveries from dominating the data.

  • A settings tokenobservable plant

    The teacher now reads 64 values describing the vehicle it is flying: mass, inertia, motors, drag, wind and gusts, delay and actuator limits. They enter as one extra token with a direct attention path and a zero-initialized FiLM modulation of every other token. The model grows 6.4%, from 5.49 M to 5.84 M parameters. The token was first added to the old recipe on its own, in the grey run of Figure 5, and there it did not produce the speed-up.

  • Replay and objectivefitting choices

    Batches draw 90% from recent online data and 10% from a permanent expert pool, where the earlier run drew 65% and 35%. Three times as many new rows are kept per round, over a horizon of about 15 rounds, so old rows are reused less. Yaw-rate error is weighted at 0.35 of the other axes, and the learning rate is held constant.

  • Fresh statisticsnormalization

    Input normalization was recomputed from 260 new expert episodes under the corrected contract, and the model was trained from scratch rather than fine-tuned across the change.

Behavioral analysis

Pace

Across the 2,949 completed real100-v2 laps, the median lap takes 10.3 s and 1.18× the course’s expert reference time; 90% finish within 1.62×. The reference is an empirical witness from the expert, not a certified optimum: 16 laps took less than half of it. Clean, on-time laps are the fastest group, and most of the slow tail is laps with a missed gate.

A speed command that means something

The policy is told how fast to fly. On a paired 16-course panel, raising the command from the nominal 16.5 to 21 m/s made laps about 6% faster on the starts that completed at both speeds, with completion steady. At 26 m/s, completion collapsed to 23%. Lower commands were slower but not more reliable at the strictest bar. The command changes pace in a useful band, and is not a free route to speed.

Missed gates and recoveries

A gate only counts when it is crossed through its aperture, in order and in the direction of travel. After a miss the gate stays the active target, and nothing at deployment scripts what happens next. There is no recovery controller and no reset. The policy turns back and takes it again, and that behavior is learned from the expert’s corrective labels on states the learner reached.

Recoveries are the main quality cost. 677 completions came after a detected miss. 116 of those recovered more than one gate, and 60 missed the same gate more than once. Most recoveries are short, but the tail is long.

Four benchmark episodes, chosen to show the range: a short recovery on a go-around course, a 38.65 s late rescue, and both an A2RL recovery and an A2RL failure. Orange marks the stretch from the missed crossing to the successful one.

What it does not do yet

The strongest accurate description is a highly capable pretrained base with remaining weaknesses in first-pass execution, pacing and a few outlier courses. Completion is approaching a ceiling on many courses. Clean, on-time flight is not.

First-pass execution

747 completions fall short of clean and on time, 677 of them after a missed gate. 22 courses are clean and on time on all 32 starts, and 11 are never clean and on time, some while completing nearly every start.

Public reference courses

The eight public adaptations complete 59.4% as a family. A2RL finishes 4 of 32 starts, all after misses, and UTT04 and UTT06 finish 0 and 1. A slower command did not fix A2RL (1 of 32 at 6 m/s). Longer ordered gate chains sit measurably further from the training geometry than single transitions do.

Long rescues

One lap spent 32.2 s getting back to a single missed gate, finishing after 38.7 s and 308 m. 87 completions spent more than 8 s on one recovery. The undesired behavior was reduced enough for strong fitting and transfer, but not eliminated.

Evaluation boundaries. All results are in simulation, from a policy given privileged true state, route and vehicle parameters. The public reference courses are reconstructions, not surveyed venues, and their times are not leaderboard times. Both held-out panels were inspected during the research, and the selection panel was reused to rank checkpoints. Each recipe is one training seed. The run was planned for 218 rounds and ended at round 93, so the selected round-71 checkpoint is the best measured base, not a converged one. The gallery is curated.

Method

Flying a course at speed is a control problem with a moving reference. The policy has to commit to a line through the next several gates while its own attitude and rates change underneath it, and it has to do that from whatever state the last maneuver left it in. Everything the policy sees is expressed in the frame of the gate it is currently flying at, so the same maneuver looks the same wherever it appears on a course.

Architecture

One causal transformer reads fourteen tokens: the settings token, three steps of state and control history, the six gates ahead, and a learned readout token. Position is encoded in what each token is: a gate is only ever readable through the slot for its place in the route, so the network holds a representation of “the gate two ahead” directly. Both heads read the single readout token. The dynamics head predicts the one-step change in task state during training and is discarded afterwards; it exists to shape the shared latent.

Pretraining

Every label comes from an MPCC expert solved with acados. It labels each visited state with the first command of its own plan, so the policy learns the expert’s intent at the states the learner itself reaches.

A different vehicle every episode

At every reset the simulator samples a new plant and holds it fixed for the episode. The teacher reads it through the settings token; the student will not, and must infer it.

From privileged teacher to deployable student

Everything above is the first stage: a privileged teacher that reads true vehicle state, exact gate geometry and the sampled plant, none of which a real racing drone gets. Privileged inputs make the teacher cheap to train and easy to analyze, and its job is to be distilled.

The second stage is a single student with a normal observation: raw or estimated state plus vision tokens. A forward-facing camera’s gate segmentation is cut into patches and projected into tokens at every history step, so the state values can attend to what the camera sees. In training the masks come from the simulator’s ground truth, with noise and augmentation. The student is not given the plant, so alongside the dynamics head it carries an auxiliary head that predicts the plant parameters. The aim is one student that flies many vehicles, with the commanded speed still adjustable at test time.

Status. A fresh distillation from this checkpoint is next. An earlier distillation from the previous teacher was affected by a known bug and is not used as evidence of student capability. The student will be evaluated paired with this teacher on identical courses, starts, vehicles, commands and deadlines. We will report absolute eventual, timely and clean-and-timely rates beside the teacher’s, then retention ratios, lap distributions and the hard reference courses.

Why no reinforcement learning here

Policy-gradient reinforcement learning is the established route to the strongest high-speed racing controllers, including champion-level drone racing, and RL comes next in this line of work. This paper leaves it out on purpose, to isolate one question: how much of a general flight controller can be learned as a base model, from supervision alone.

Every behavior on this page came from supervised labels, from flying held-out layouts to turning back through a missed gate, so none of it can be attributed to reward shaping. It also sets up the RL stage well. A randomly initialized controller spends its early experience learning to stay airborne and find the gates at all. A base that already completes 92% of hard held-out starts and responds to a speed command lets RL spend its samples on what is left.

Supervision builds the skill

Dense per-state expert labels teach the hard part cheaply: committing to a line through ordered gates, and coming back from the wrong side of one.

The representation is shaped

Auxiliary dynamics and, in the student, plant heads give downstream learning a latent that carries local dynamics, the route ahead and the vehicle.

The targets are measured

The 23-point gap between completion and clean-and-on-time flight, the recovery tail, and the hardest reference courses are specific things for RL to improve.

Claim boundary. We have not run a matched comparison against RL from random initialization. These numbers show substantial ability before RL; they do not establish that RL from this starting point is more sample-efficient or reaches better final performance than RL from scratch.

Experiments in progress

  1. 1 · Distillation into the vision student

    Setup. Distill this checkpoint into the student three ways: no auxiliary head, dynamics head alone, and dynamics plus plant prediction. Evaluate each paired with the teacher on real100-v2 and real60.

    Question. How much completion, pace and first-pass quality survives the loss of privileged state and plant, and does an explicit plant head make the vehicle readable from the student’s latent?

  2. 2 · Attributing the speed-up

    Setup. A matched factorial study over the pieces bundled in this recipe: the sample-row repairs, learner-flown segments, the settings token, and the replay and objective changes.

    Question. Which of them buys the order-of-magnitude faster learning, and do they interact?

  3. 3 · First-pass execution and pace

    Setup. Target clean, on-time flight directly: continued pretraining against a midtraining objective aimed at the first attempt at each gate, and a within-course speed curriculum that breaks the tie between commanded speed and course type.

    Question. Can the 23-point gap between completion and clean-and-on-time flight be closed before RL, and does the speed command become purer pace when it is no longer correlated with the kind of course?
