Learning agile flight

Starscream studies learned control for agile drone flight. Policies use vehicle state, recent actions, and upcoming gate geometry to produce thrust and body-rate commands. The experiments examine expert-supervised training, controller architecture, and recovery across a diverse set of racing courses.

Reinforcement learning experiments are underway. The results below come from the existing three-seed expert-supervised comparison in simulation, with state and gate geometry supplied directly to the policy.

Expert supervision

The expert predicts motion with a dynamics model and optimizes a sequence of controls over a one-second horizon. It tracks a course reference and speed profile under actuator limits, limits on control changes, and corridor constraints. It applies the first control and replans. The displayed results use the historical profile-tracking MPC configuration, named MPCC in the implementation. Newer solver work is separate from this comparison.

The policy learns the expert’s state-to-action mapping from labels on states it visits. During collection, it flies segments of two to four gates while the solver labels each visited state, including departures from the reference. Training mixes recent experience with expert examples and includes an auxiliary prediction of the transition caused by the executed action. The trained policy then flies complete evaluation courses on its own.

Privileged state

The policy receives gate-frame position and velocity, orientation, body rates, motor speeds, and vehicle context directly from the simulator.

Six upcoming gates

Ordered gate geometry, apertures, and validity let the policy account for later approaches when choosing the current action.

State and action history

Three rows span about 15.4 ms at 130 Hz. Previously executed controls provide context for the plant’s 11 ms actuation delay.

Course geometry

Training uses pretrain65, a set of 65 qualified courses. eval100 combines 92 generated courses with eight adaptations of public track layouts across 15 families. These include slalom, hairpins, reversals, long braking approaches, vertical chains, and oblique gate entries. Each course contains 5–19 gates, so the controller must link several maneuvers while setting up the next approach.

Gate-center spacing ranges from 1.6 to 47.4 m. Short gaps leave little time to correct an approach; longer gaps allow acceleration followed by braking for the next gate. 78 courses include a change of at least 120° between incoming and outgoing gate-center directions, requiring the controller to coordinate braking and lateral acceleration. 54 courses include a height change of at least 2 m between gates, adding climb or descent to the same sequence. These combinations make anticipation useful: the action that passes one gate also determines the speed, attitude, and position available for the next.

eval100 contains the 25-course checkpoint-selection subset and was inspected during development. All flights are simulated. eval60 names a separate 60-course suite; the recovery panel below uses 60 starts across 15 eval100 courses.

Expert-supervised controller comparison

MLP, LSTM, and transformer setups each have about 5.84M parameters and three independently trained seeds. All receive short state/action history, route context, vehicle information, and a speed command:

MLP

Projects the three history rows and concatenates them for temporal fusion.

LSTM

Processes the three-row window with hidden and cell state reset on each call.

Transformer

Uses 14 typed tokens: plant settings, three state/control pairs, six gates, and an action readout attending to the preceding tokens.

Each selected policy flies 12 assigned starts on every eval100 course, giving 1,200 trials per seed. We report three outcomes:

Clean & Timely
Finish without a reference-aware missed gate and within the course deadline.
Clean
Finish without a missed gate, even if late.
Eventual
Finish all gates in order without crashing, within the 46.154 s hard cap; recovery after a miss is allowed.

Bars: mean across three training seeds. Dots: individual seeds. Labels: mean ± sample SD. All rates divide by assigned starts, including crashes and timeouts.

In this expert-supervised comparison, the transformer setup reaches 66.7% Clean & Timely, compared with 52.5% for LSTM and 50.1% for MLP. The difference exceeds the observed seed spread. All three finish substantially more courses than they complete cleanly, showing how often eventual completion includes a missed gate and recovery.

Inputs and training budgets differ across setups. MLP and transformer receive 54 explicit approach features, and the models handle route and vehicle history differently. Isolating the contribution of attention requires matched ablations.

Recovery after a disturbance

The recovery panel starts the policy with a 1.5 m lateral offset and 2 m/s lateral velocity. It uses four starts on one course from each of 15 families, giving 60 assigned starts per seed. MLP rejoins the first gate in 87.8% of starts; LSTM and transformer each reach 96.1%. Full-course outcomes show how much quality survives after that rejoin.

Means and sample SD across three training seeds, using the same outcome definitions as the nominal comparison. Each seed has 60 assigned recovery starts.

Clean & Timely completion is similar across the three setups, at 31–32%. The controlled-recovery results therefore offer little separation despite the transformer’s stronger nominal score. Recovery after naturally occurring misses needs a separate interpretation because each policy produces its own set of missed-gate states.
