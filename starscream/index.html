Starscream Sample-efficient generalization for high-speed agile drone racing

Andrew Olvera

York College of Pennsylvania

Paper (coming soon) Results

Abstract

We introduce a hybrid DAgger + DART and dynamics-learning pretraining method for scaling generalization of high-speed agile drone racing with extreme sample efficiency. We also introduce a causal decoder transformer architecture leveraging a sequence design that forces geometric continuous trajectory conditioning. We achieve high accuracy and extreme generalization at high speeds completely from supervised labels produced by an MPCC teacher controller, enabling distillation of privileged, scripted complex behavior at scale.

The setup

Flying a course at speed is a control problem with a moving reference. The policy has to commit to a line through the next several gates while its own attitude and rates are changing underneath it, and it has to do that from whatever state the last manoeuvre left it in.

Everything it sees is expressed in the frame of the gate it is currently flying at, so the same manoeuvre looks the same wherever it appears on a course. Each group of fields gets its own projection, which keeps one token meaning one thing.

Architecture

The actor is one causal transformer reading thirteen tokens: three steps of state and control history, the six gates ahead, and a learned readout token.

Position is carried by what a token is, not by where it sits. A gate is only ever readable through the slot for its place in the route, so “the gate two ahead” becomes something the network holds a representation of rather than an offset it has to recover from an index. The ordering of the route is structural, and the continuous line through it is what the readout has to attend over. That is where the geometric conditioning comes from.

Everything the policy decides is formed at that one readout token. Both heads read it, which forces a single representation to answer two different questions: what to command now, and where the vehicle will be a step from now. The dynamics head is thrown away after training, but the pressure it puts on the shared latent is the reason it is there.

Pretraining

Every label comes from an MPCC expert solved with acados. It labels each visited state with the first command of its own plan, so the policy learns the teacher’s intent rather than whatever the rollout happened to execute. That one choice is what lets DAgger, DART noise and expert-prefix roll-ins all write into the same replay without contradicting each other.

It also fixes the dynamics target. The auxiliary predicts the state MPCC expects after its own first command, not the transition that actually occurred, so the target stays correct when the learner or the injected noise chose the executed action, and at gate crossings when the frame switches underneath it.

Every rollout on this page comes from that one run. The shape of those numbers is the interesting part: a 5.49 M-parameter policy is pushed through more than a million updates against a pool of states that is small by comparison, because very little of what the rollouts produce ever becomes a label. The expert has to solve at every visited state, failed solves and crashed episodes are discarded outright, and a third of every batch is reserved for clean expert behaviour.

What survives that filtering is expensive, so it gets used hard. Spending this much compute per retained sample is the trade being made, and it is what buys the sample efficiency.

Design decisions

  • The loss is computed twice, once on normalized CTBR and once in physical units scaled by [10, 3, 3, 3], so thrust error and rate error stay comparable instead of one drowning out the other.
  • A third of every batch is drawn from the permanent expert pool, so clean expert behaviour never washes out as the online replay fills with the policy’s own mistakes.
  • Failed solves and crashed episodes are dropped rather than labelled. The policy is never asked to imitate a plan the expert could not fly.

Behavioral analysis

The rollouts above are the clean ones. The more telling behaviour is what the policy does when a gate approach goes wrong. We tagged seven rollouts in which the policy recovers from a missed or aborted approach and still finishes the course, and located each recovery in the recorded states.

A recovery leg is a gate-to-gate leg, inside a tagged rollout, that flies at least 5 m further than the straight line and either takes twice that rollout’s median leg time or has a detour ratio (path length over straight-line distance) of at least 3. Recovery legs are drawn in orange.

Detours, leg by leg

Every gate-to-gate leg in all nineteen rollouts, placed by its detour ratio. Clean legs sit close to 1 regardless of course. The worst recoveries sit far outside anything in clean flight; the milder ones overlap it on detour alone and are flagged by the time they take instead.

What the recoveries look like

  • It brakes rather than forcing the gate. The slowest point of a recovery leg drops to between 0.6 and 3.8 m/s, against a median of 7.4 m/s for the slowest point of a leg in the fastest set.
  • It goes around and takes the gate again. Recovery legs cost a median of about 21 m of extra path, and every tagged rollout still clears every gate in order.
  • The rest of the rollout is untouched. Outside the recovery legs, tagged rollouts fly with a median detour of 1.01, no looser than the fastest set (1.07). The recovery is local, not a degraded mode.
  • Same course, different episodes. Flow 027-057 appears twice: one episode recovers once at gate 10, the other three times (gates 5, 9 and 18) and takes more than twice as long, yet both finish.
