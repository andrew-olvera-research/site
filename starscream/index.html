Starscream A pretrained, course- and vehicle-general policy for agile drone racing

Andrew Olvera

York College of Pennsylvania

Paper (coming soon) Results

Abstract

We pretrain an agile drone-racing base model entirely from supervised labels, with no reinforcement learning, to isolate what can be learned as representation before any policy-gradient fine-tuning. An MPCC expert labels the states the learner actually reaches (DAgger), DART noise and expert-prefix roll-ins widen where those states come from, and an auxiliary one-step dynamics head shapes the representation the policy acts from. The policy is a 5.49 M-parameter causal transformer that reads the next six gates as ordered route tokens and takes a commanded speed, trained on the expert’s time-optimal pace for each course and adjustable at test time. Every episode flies a freshly sampled vehicle, with randomized mass, inertia, thrust mapping, actuator limits, aerodynamic response, wind and center-of-mass offset. In the run reported here the policy was not given those parameters, and still, before any RL, it completes 60.6% of attempts on 52 unseen course layouts and 45.5% on 92 unseen hard layouts, and turns back to take a gate it has missed. The full method treats this model as a privileged teacher that sees the plant, distilled into deployment-observation and vision-based students that do not and must infer it, with plant prediction as an auxiliary target. We report the teacher results, the experiments now running, and what the base model does and does not yet show; final results will be released with the preprint.

The setup

Flying a course at speed is a control problem with a moving reference. The policy has to commit to a line through the next several gates while its own attitude and rates are changing underneath it, and it has to do that from whatever state the last manoeuvre left it in.

Everything it sees is expressed in the frame of the gate it is currently flying at, so the same manoeuvre looks the same wherever it appears on a course. Each group of fields gets its own projection, which keeps one token meaning one thing.

A different vehicle every episode

A course is only half of what changes. At every reset the simulator samples a new plant and holds it fixed for that episode, so the policy never flies the same vehicle twice in a row. In the method as designed, the privileged teacher is given the sampled parameters and the student is not. In the v6.21.1 run reported on this page, randomization was on but the parameters were not in the teacher’s observation (see the note on this run), so every teacher result here comes from a policy that had to act on vehicles it was not told about.

Speed as an input

How fast to fly is not left for the network to guess. For each course, the MPCC expert’s plan fixes a time-optimal average speed, and that number is given to the policy as a commanded speed, v_cmd. It enters after the backbone through a zero-initialized conditioner, so the features that read the route and the vehicle are shared across speeds.

Because it is an input, it can be changed at test time, and the policy generalizes to values it was not trained on for that course. Raising it above the nominal value has non-trivial effects on how the policy flies, and on some courses a higher command even raises completion.

That second effect suggests the command carries more than a speed. In training, the courses with high time-optimal speeds tend to be open ones, with fewer gates and fewer transitions, and they are also the courses the policy completes most often; tight, technical courses with many gates get low speed targets and complete less often. A high v_cmd may therefore also select the behavior mode learned on open, high-success courses. We hypothesize that the command conditions a mode of flying, and with it a success rate, as well as a pace. Experiment 3 tests this directly.

Architecture

The actor is one causal transformer reading thirteen tokens: three steps of state and control history, the six gates ahead, and a learned readout token.

Position is carried by what a token is, not by where it sits. A gate is only ever readable through the slot for its place in the route, so “the gate two ahead” becomes something the network holds a representation of rather than an offset it has to recover from an index. The ordering of the route is structural, and the continuous line through it is what the readout has to attend over. That is where the geometric conditioning comes from.

Everything the policy decides is formed at that one readout token. Both heads read it, which forces a single representation to answer two different questions: what to command now, and where the vehicle will be a step from now. The dynamics head is thrown away after training, but the pressure it puts on the shared latent is the reason it is there.

Pretraining

Every label comes from an MPCC expert solved with acados. It labels each visited state with the first command of its own plan, so the policy learns the teacher’s intent rather than whatever the rollout happened to execute. That one choice is what lets DAgger, DART noise and expert-prefix roll-ins all write into the same replay without contradicting each other.

It also fixes the dynamics target. The auxiliary predicts the state MPCC expects after its own first command, not the transition that actually occurred, so the target stays correct when the learner or the injected noise chose the executed action, and at gate crossings when the frame switches underneath it.

Every rollout on this page comes from that one run. The shape of those numbers is the interesting part: a 5.49 M-parameter policy is pushed through more than a million updates against a pool of states that is small by comparison, because very little of what the rollouts produce ever becomes a label. The expert has to solve at every visited state, failed solves and crashed episodes are discarded outright, and a third of every batch is reserved for clean expert behaviour.

What survives that filtering is expensive, so it gets used hard. Spending this much compute per retained sample is the trade being made, and it is what buys the sample efficiency.

Design decisions

  • The loss is computed twice, once on normalized CTBR and once in physical units scaled by [10, 3, 3, 3], so thrust error and rate error stay comparable instead of one drowning out the other.
  • A third of every batch is drawn from the permanent expert pool, so clean expert behaviour never washes out as the online replay fills with the policy’s own mistakes.
  • Failed solves and crashed episodes are dropped rather than labelled. The policy is never asked to imitate a plan the expert could not fly.

Results

Everything in this section comes from a single checkpoint: the pretrained actor selected at 121,343,545 collected environment steps, before any reinforcement learning. It flies from privileged true vehicle state, the ordered next six gates, three steps of history, its own previous commands and a commanded speed, and it outputs one collective-thrust-and-body-rate command per control step. In this run it was not given the parameters of the vehicle it was flying.

Read plainly: on a layout it has never seen, flying a vehicle it has not been told about, the pretrained policy finishes between 45% and 61% of its attempts, more often on the easier suite than the hard one. That is a capable policy, not a solved problem. Two caveats keep the numbers honest. The hard suite is benchmark-informed development geometry, not a protected final test. And the eight public reference courses that both suites share are reported separately: the policy completed 15 of 64 attempts there, so earlier exposure to those layouts did not make them easy, and they are excluded from the fresh-geometry rates above.

Where it succeeds, and where it does not

Completion depends far more on the kind of route than on the suite. Courses that turn back through gates are flown most reliably; long, low courses mostly are not.

The spread is the useful part. The families flown best (go-around, compound reversal, ordered 3D) are ones where the route doubles back or changes height across several gates, which is where reading the ordered gates ahead should matter most. That is consistent with the design, though we have not tested it directly. Long-low courses complete at only 18.8% and 6.2%. We have not isolated why; they are the clearest target for what comes after pretraining.

Behavioral analysis

The clean laps show that the policy can fly a route. The more revealing question is what it does when a gate goes wrong.

A gate only counts when the vehicle crosses it through its directed aperture: inside the frame, in the direction of travel. Miss it, and the course does not advance. The missed gate stays the active target, stays in the first lookahead slot, and remains the frame everything else is expressed in. Nothing at deployment scripts what happens next: there is no recovery controller and no reset. Whatever the vehicle does is the pretrained actor alone.

What it does, in every case we recorded, is the same thing a pilot would do. It turns back to the approach side of the gate it missed and takes the gate again.

Two recoveries, read from the traces

On Go-around 017-040, the actor missed the last gate’s aperture, returned to its approach side, and crossed successfully about 1.2 s later. On Long low braking 003-08, it missed the second gate twice, returned to the approach side after each miss, and eventually crossed; later in the same lap it did the same after missing the seventh gate. A matched clean replay had no missed crossings, and the gate tracker never advanced on a miss. These are selected examples, not an estimate of how often the policy recovers.

Locating every recovery

To find recoveries without relying on how the rollouts were tagged, we look for one signature in the recorded states: the vehicle crosses its target gate’s plane near the gate, but the gate does not register. That is a crossing outside the aperture or in the wrong direction, and it forces a return. Across all nineteen rollouts the test flags ten gate-to-gate legs. All ten fall in the seven rollouts tagged as recoveries, and none of the 92 legs in the fastest set trips it. Recovery legs are drawn in orange throughout.

What the recoveries have in common

  • It comes back through the plane. In every flagged leg the vehicle crosses back to the approach side of the missed gate before taking it. It does not skip the gate or chase the next one.
  • It sheds speed to do it. The slowest point of a recovery leg is between 0.6 and 3.4 m/s. For comparison, the slowest point of a typical leg in the fastest set is 7.4 m/s.
  • It costs distance, not the lap. A recovery adds a median of about 22 m of path and 4.4 s, and every tagged rollout still clears every gate in order.
  • The rest of the lap looks clean. Away from the missed gate, tagged rollouts fly with a median detour of 1.01, no looser than the fastest set (1.07). Behaviorally, the disruption stays local to the gate that was missed.
  • Same course, different episodes. Flow 027-057 appears twice. One episode recovers once, at gate 10; the other recovers three times, at gates 5, 9 and 18, and takes more than twice as long. Both finish.

Where the behavior likely comes from

No part of training was a dedicated recovery teacher. The policy learned from one MPCC expert, in closed loop. DAgger queried that expert on the states the learner itself reached, early DART noise and expert-prefix handoffs pushed those states further from the clean line, and whenever the learner wandered off-line or onto the wrong side of a gate, the same expert labelled the way back. The most plausible account is that recovery was learned from those corrective labels, as ordinary imitation of the expert from unusual states.

We are careful about how much weight that account can carry. The recipe requested a 40/35/25 split of nominal, critical and recovery states in replay, but those are sampling weights; they do not establish that a quarter of the labels were recoveries. In three saved replay shards we inspected, about half the rows come from expert-prefix roll-ins, and none carries a separate recovery-teacher tag. Assigning a causal share of this behavior to corrective supervision needs an ablation that removes those labels.

What the representation carries

Two measurements speak to what the shared latent at the readout token holds. Both are small and exploratory; we report them because they point the same way.

R² 0.48one-step dynamics readout, pooled over 4,356 control steps on three hard-course laps
0.67 · 0.57 · 0.16mean L2 change in the normalized command when only the route beyond the last gate is altered

It carries the local dynamics. The dynamics head reads the same token the action head reads. Out of sample, on three rendered hard-course laps, it predicts the normalized next change in task state with a pooled R² of 0.483 (gate-frame transitions excluded). Roughly half the variance of the next task-state change is recoverable from the representation the policy acts from. The laps were selected successful ones, so this is evidence, not a population estimate.

It reads the route ahead, not just the next gate. At the end of a lap, the lookahead slots past the final gate are padded by repeating it. Holding the vehicle state fixed, we replaced that padding with a synthetic straight-through route. The command moved, by mean L2 distances of 0.67, 0.57 and 0.16 on the three laps. Gates the vehicle is not yet flying at change what it does now. This is an offline input intervention; it shows the future slots matter, not that the altered route would fly better.

Together with the recoveries, the picture is of ordered, route-conditioned control: a policy whose command depends on where it is relative to the active gate, how it is moving, and the ordered route beyond, and which holds enough of the local dynamics to turn a miss into a second approach.

Not claimed. These results do not show a discrete “recovery mode” in the latent, an explicit model of which states are still viable, or state estimation: the policy is given true state. Nor do they show topological understanding of a course. Each of these is a hypothesis for a targeted ablation or probe.

From privileged teacher to deployable student

The method has two stages. The first is the pretrained model on this page: a privileged teacher. It reads true vehicle state and exact gate geometry, which a real racing drone does not get. That is deliberate. Privileged inputs make the teacher cheap to train and easy to analyse, and its job is to be distilled. By design, the teacher also reads the sampled plant parameters of the vehicle it is flying, so that it can learn the best way to fly each vehicle without having to discover which one it has.

The second stage is a student that does not get any of that. One student flies from deployment observations, the signals an onboard stack actually has; another flies from vision. The teacher supplies the targets, as the MPCC expert did for the teacher. The student is not given the plant, so it has to infer from its own recent observations and commands what the teacher was told outright. We make that explicit: alongside the one-step dynamics head, the student carries an auxiliary head that predicts the plant parameters, so the representation it acts from is pushed to carry an estimate of the vehicle it is flying. The result is meant to be one student that flies many vehicles of different weight and capability, with the commanded speed still adjustable at test time.

The first distillation runs carry meaningful success rate and speed over from the teacher to both the deployment-observation and the vision-based student. Full numbers will be released with the preprint.

A note on the run reported here. In the v6.21.1 run behind every teacher result on this page, plant randomization was left enabled by mistake, and the plant parameters were not part of the teacher’s observation. We report it as it ran. It means those results were obtained under a harder condition than the method intends: a policy that flies unseen courses at 45 to 61% completion without being told which vehicle it has, from true state, three steps of history and its own previous commands. That the architecture copes with an unobserved plant at all is itself informative. Whether giving the teacher the plant helps or changes what it learns is exactly what the first experiment below measures.

Why no reinforcement learning here

Policy-gradient reinforcement learning is the established route to the strongest high-speed, high-accuracy racing controllers, including champion-level drone racing. We do not dispute that, and RL comes next in this line of work. This paper leaves it out on purpose, to isolate one question: how much of a general flight controller can be learned as a base model, from supervision alone, and what that model’s representation carries.

Keeping RL out keeps the answer clean. Every behavior on this page, from flying unseen layouts to turning back through a missed gate, came from supervised labels, so none of it can be attributed to reward shaping or policy-gradient fine-tuning. It also sets up the RL stage well. Racing is unforgiving: progress comes only from crossing gates in order, at speed, and one wrong command at 16 m/s ends the episode. A randomly initialized controller spends its early experience learning to stay airborne and to find the gates at all. A base model that already completes 45% to 61% of unseen layouts, recovers from missed gates and responds to a test-time speed command lets RL spend its samples on pace and consistency instead.

Supervision builds the skill

Dense, per-state expert labels teach the hard part cheaply: how to commit to a line through ordered gates, and how to come back from the wrong side of one. No reward shaping is involved.

The representation is shaped

Auxiliary heads for dynamics, and in the student for the plant, give downstream learning a latent that carries local dynamics, the route ahead and the vehicle, instead of one it has to build from reward alone.

The targets are measured

Long-low courses (18.8% and 6.2%), consistency across vehicles, and the cost of a recovery (a median of about 22 m of extra path) are specific, measurable things for RL to improve.

Claim boundary. We have not run a matched comparison against RL from random initialization. The numbers here show substantial ability before RL; they do not establish that RL from this starting point is more sample-efficient, or reaches better final performance, than RL from scratch.

Experiments in progress

Three controlled experiments turn the story above into measured claims. Each changes one thing against a matched baseline: same courses, seeds, plant randomization, training budget and evaluation suites.

  1. 1 · Plant in the teacher’s observation

    Setup. Retrain the teacher with the sampled plant parameters added to its privileged observation, against the as-run v6.21.1 recipe without them. Compare completion on fresh geometry, lap time, recovery behavior and learning speed, broken down by where each vehicle sits in Table 1.

    Hypothesis. Giving the plant makes the teacher at least as good, most visibly on vehicles near the edges of the range (heavy, weak-thrust, high-drag). A small gap would say the architecture already infers most of what it needs from its history; a large one would say the as-run results were paid for with capacity spent on inference, and that the privileged teacher is the better base to distill from.

  2. 2 · Distillation with a plant head

    Setup. Distill the privileged teacher into deployment-observation and vision-based students with no auxiliary head, the dynamics head alone, and dynamics plus plant prediction. Measure the success rate and speed each student retains from the teacher, per-vehicle performance across Table 1, and how accurately each plant parameter can be read from the student’s latent.

    Hypothesis. The plant head improves transfer on hard vehicles and makes the vehicle explicitly readable from the student’s representation, where the dynamics head alone carries it only implicitly.

  3. 3 · What the speed command selects

    Setup. Sweep the commanded speed below and above each course’s nominal value, for the teacher and for each student, and measure completion, lap time and flight behavior separately for open, low-gate-count courses and tight, technical ones. As a control, train a teacher on per-episode rescaled speed targets, so that the command is no longer correlated with the kind of course.

    Hypothesis. Speed and mode are entangled in the as-run model: raising the command pulls technical courses toward the behavior learned on open, high-success courses, which is why completion can rise with it. With the correlation broken in training, the command should act more purely as pace, trading completion for lap time. Either way, the response should survive distillation, so a deployed student keeps a speed command that means something.

Final results for all three, together with the student evaluations, will be released with the preprint.
