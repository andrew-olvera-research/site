MicroRTS research report

Learning under fog of war

Andrew Olvera

York College of Pennsylvania

Main result Experiment records Code

In MicroRTS, a player issues commands to multiple units while seeing only the parts of the map those units can observe. I used that setting to study a question that matters for autonomous systems with incomplete information: can access to the full state during training help a policy that acts from local observations?

The experiments separate two outcomes. In a short policy-learning comparison, a pretrained critic with access to the full board fit its value targets more closely. None of the four arms improved on its starting win count on the development panel. In a separate study, a model trained to infer an opponent belief from visible play history predicted some longer-horizon returns more accurately.

What the policy could see

The GridNet policy received a fog-projected board and produced commands for its units. All four arms used the same action-masking adapter, which derives legal-action masks from simulator state. The experiment restricts the policy’s observation tensor to the visible board. It leaves open whether the masks can reveal differences between otherwise identical fog observations.

The critic estimates a value used to train the policy. I compared an independently initialized critic that saw the fog observation with a separately pretrained critic that saw the full state. I also tested whether adding four-step rollouts from a learned world model helped. All arms learned from real simulator transitions and shared the same dense reward recipe, actor initialization, and real-interaction budget. The experiment changes critic architecture and pretraining along with state access. The imagined arms also received additional actor updates.

Better value fit did not carry over to play

Each arm ran two training seeds for 262,144 real learner transitions per seed. At every checkpoint, each seed played 16 greedy evaluation games across two maps, four opponents, and both player seats. The final column uses the checkpoint at the end of the fixed budget.

The value losses use each arm’s own evolving data and bootstrapped targets. They measure training fit and leave calibration and action ranking open. All 14 final wins came from seat 0 on the base map. The development panel had some outcome variation even with identical starting actor weights. Across all 128 final games, 19 reached the time limit and counted as neither wins nor losses.

I also checked what the policies chose on 640 shared states from 64 held-out games. Across 1,335 actionable unit decisions per actor, movement fell from 40.4% at initialization to 31.2–34.7% across the four arms. These were intended commands scored on the same observations and masks. Their consequences in live games remain an open question.

A separate world-model qualification illustrates where aggregate prediction error can hide a problem. Mean absolute reward error was 0.400 over 4,944 labels and 7.446 on the 112 terminal labels. Those measurements come from a separate qualification panel and do not identify the cause of the policy results.

Tells: learning an opponent belief from history

Tells investigates what the player can infer from its own sequence of observations and past commands. A training-time teacher with privileged information learns a set of behavior codes. A causal history encoder estimates a distribution over those codes from fog observations and the player’s past actions. A return head uses that inferred distribution to predict future shaped returns.

In historical single-seed validation summaries, conditioning on the inferred belief reduced smooth-L1 loss in symlog coordinates from 0.1634 to 0.1537 at 32 steps and from 0.3236 to 0.3134 at 128 steps, compared with an independently trained state-only head. The one-step loss increased slightly. The comparison is modest, and the historical policy panels leave the value of belief conditioning for control unresolved.

Research software and evidence

The training stack pairs a vectorized Java simulator with GPU policy updates, supports cloned simulator branches for targeted checks, and tracks departures, arrivals, and episode endpoints separately. In a bounded matched benchmark at 128 learner pairs, returning only the structured fields consumed by the learner raised mean throughput from 229.5 to 239.3 real learner transitions per second across two repeats per setting. That measurement covers 80 ticks per repeat and excludes evaluation and checkpoint I/O.

The next question is whether either source of additional information improves the ranking of actions at the same decision point. That requires comparing predictions with outcomes from matched simulator branches and then testing the selected policy across independent maps, both seats, and multiple training seeds.
