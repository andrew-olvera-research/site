Tells Inferring an opponent from play history in a MicroRTS world action model

Andrew Olvera

York College of Pennsylvania

Paper (coming soon) Results

Abstract

A poker player never sees the other hand; they read tells. We ask whether a model-based agent in MicroRTS can do the same under fog of war: infer a useful opponent behavior mode from information legally available during play, use it in prediction and value estimation, and win more because of it. We first build an auditable action-conditioned latent simulator from full engine state, then move its deployment interface to fog-visible observations and the agent’s own issued actions. A structured tokenizer with exact categorical decoding and temporal JEPA reconstructs 99.85% of held-out frames exactly. A 103.1M-parameter flow dynamics model learns strong one-step semantic and counterfactual prediction (0.973 changed-cell F1), although only 11.7% of its predicted frames are exact. A 64-frame causal encoder distills a behavior-grounded 16-code opponent mode from privileged training targets, and that belief measurably improves long-horizon return prediction. Policies trained by PMPO in the frozen model beat the held-out CoacAI bot in small match panels, but the strongest of them uses no opponent belief at all, and matched belief-conditioned PMPO did not outperform it. We report where opponent belief improved prediction, and why that has not yet translated into better control.

The question

Real-time strategy is a game of hidden information. Most of the map is dark, and the opponent’s plan is visible only through what it has already done: where its workers went, what it built, when it attacked. A strong player carries a running read of the opponent, a belief about what kind of game they are in, and plays against that belief rather than against the board alone.

This project builds that read into a world action model: a latent transition queried with the agent’s own issued action, in which the opponent is hidden state to be inferred. The work is diagnostic by design. It measures separately whether the state is encoded exactly, whether the model responds to the agent’s actions, whether it predicts the effect of an intervention, whether the opponent belief carries long-horizon information, and whether any of that wins games.

Data built for interventions

MicroRTS is played on 16×16 maps. Each cell carries 27 one-hot channels (hit points, resources, owner, unit type, current action), and a visibility mask separates what is observed from what is hidden. A unit command has eight fields, from source cell and action type to produced unit and relative attack target, and a single game step can issue many of them. Raw engine unit IDs are dropped so the representation stays tied to mechanics rather than to unstable identifiers.

The corpus is designed around one question a world model has to answer: what would have happened if I had acted differently? For roughly a fifth of rows, the engine is cloned and the agent’s action is changed while the opponent’s action is held fixed. That isolates the effect of the agent’s own action without modeling how the opponent would have responded. Train, validation and evaluation use disjoint collection seeds and whole episodes, across four map variants, both seats, self-play and scripted opponents. CoacAI, the hard scripted opponent used for the headline comparison, appears only in evaluation.

A world model that is exact where it can be

Tokenizing a board without losing it

A game board is mostly empty, so average cell accuracy is misleading. An early JEPA tokenizer scored 94.9% on cells overall, yet got only a quarter of occupied cells exactly right and never reconstructed a whole frame. The fix was to stop treating the board as an image. The exact tokenizer embeds every finite game field categorically and decodes it back by argmax, stores when each unit’s current action began as a bounded action age recovered with the global clock, and weights changed cells 8× in its temporal JEPA objective. Its latent stays continuous so dynamics can operate on it; exact decoding acts as a certificate that nothing was lost.

The deployable interfaces then drop the full engine state. An ego tokenizer encodes only the fog-visible raster and visibility into 64 spatial tokens of width 320, reaching at least 99.9988% visible category accuracy on held-out data. A self-action tokenizer reconstructs every evaluated action field exactly and predicts how an issued action moves the ego latent (effect cosine 0.815).

Learning the dynamics

Several easy metrics overstate how good a dynamics model is. Most cells never change, so copying the last frame is a strong baseline, and a shaped reward can make an imagined rollout look profitable without the model ever responding to the agent’s actions. Early Dreamer-style models did exactly that: low teacher-forced loss, plausible reward correlation, and failed policies. The program therefore judged dynamics on changed-cell F1, action-shuffle gaps, counterfactual branches and real games in addition to loss.

The final 103.1M-parameter flow dynamics model predicts a residual in latent space under both the factual and the cloned-action branch, trained directly on factual, counterfactual and effect-geometry objectives. At its selected checkpoint, on a fixed held-out evaluator:

The model knows what changes and where far better than it knows the exact resulting state. The reason is specific: the tokenizer learned exact decoding, but this dynamics run put zero weight on decoded fields, so latent error can fall while a predicted point still sits on the wrong side of a categorical boundary. Adding decoded-field losses, then self-rollout consistency, is the next ablation.

Policy learning uses a separate, deployable transition: a roughly 100M-parameter model (14 blocks, width 768) trained from scratch on ego tokens and issued self actions only, with no opponent belief. One step ahead it beats copying the last frame (mechanics MSE 0.0189 against 0.0300), degrades when its actions are shuffled, and predicts reward and continuation closely (reward MAE 0.0011, continuation accuracy 1.0 on its sampled evaluation). These are one-step numbers and do not show calibrated reward over long rollouts, which is why the model is frozen during policy learning and imagination is kept to eight steps.

Reading the opponent

The obvious approach failed twice. An early four-mode conditioner collapsed onto a single hypothesis, and a later jointly trained JEPA student collapsed too: it used one code, and predicted opponent behavior no better than always guessing the average.

What worked was to define behavior first, then learn to see it. A privileged teacher, with access to full state and a short window of the opponent’s real actions, learns a 16-code conditional-policy codebook. Because each code is conditioned on the game situation, it names a local behavior or intent in context rather than a fixed player identity. A causal student then learns to predict the teacher’s soft code distribution from what the agent can legally see: up to 64 frames of fog-visible observation and its own past actions. Balancing training anchors by episode, so that long games stop dominating the target, stopped the student from collapsing.

Where the belief pays off

The belief carries little that helps predict the next 16 steps of the agent’s own latent state: shuffling it raised prediction error by only 0.40%. That is plausible, since the near future is decided mostly by what is already on the board. Its value appears over longer horizons, where the opponent’s plan has time to matter. A strategic return head trained with and without z measures this.

With the belief, the head predicts the game’s future return better at both horizons, and shuffling the belief hurts more at 128 steps than at 32. The effect is bounded. A separately trained head with no belief closes most of the gap, and across the full set of return metrics z does not uniformly dominate that state-only system. The belief is a real long-horizon signal of modest size. That justifies routing it into value estimation under control; its effect on play has to be tested in games.

From belief to control

Policies are trained with PMPO entirely inside the frozen world model, on eight-step imagined trajectories. The immediate reward head scores only arrivals; a separately validated return head, with or without z, supplies the value at the end of the imagined horizon, so an uncertain strategic signal cannot rewrite the one-step model. A frozen behavior prior keeps the actor close to commands that were actually issued in the data. Sign-balanced PMPO then pushes up actions with non-negative advantage and pushes down the rest, and the actor and critic can optionally receive the belief through FiLM conditioning.

What the matches say

  • A world-model agent can beat CoacAI. Selected PMPO checkpoints trained only in imagination won small held-out panels against it, including 5 of 5 twice for the best policy. The panels are small, and robustness has not been established.
  • The belief did not cause the wins. The strongest policy uses no belief. Its matched belief-conditioned counterpart lost 20 and drew 20 of 40 games head-to-head, with a strong seat asymmetry.
  • Good offline scores can break closed-loop play. A residual ranker that re-scores actions using the belief improved paired preference accuracy from 0.49 to 0.73 on held-out counterfactuals, and matched belief beat shuffled belief (0.733 vs 0.715). Added to the best policy at full strength it won 0 of 20; at a fifth of the strength, 8 of 20 against 13 for the unchanged policy, and CoacAI went from 5 of 5 to 0 of 5 at both scales.

What holds, and what does not yet

Exact state reconstruction, a transition that responds to the agent’s own actions, and an opponent belief with real long-horizon predictive value all work within one system, but they did not compose on their own. Exact tokenization did not force exact dynamics. Reward prediction did not guarantee calibrated imagined returns. A belief that improves return prediction did not guarantee that PMPO would use it well. Preference accuracy did not protect a ranker from collapsing in play.

The next version keeps these parts separate. Keep a validated transition that does not see the belief. Route the belief only through a separate long-horizon value or a gated residual. Require matched-versus-shuffled belief tests and real-game ablations before anything is promoted.

We obtained selected PMPO checkpoints that beat CoacAI in small held-out match panels; larger controlled evaluations are pending.

Not claimed. That the agent robustly beats CoacAI across maps, seats and seeds: five games per comparison and one seed cannot support it. That the opponent belief causes any win: the matched policy ablation contradicts it. That the dynamics model is a near-exact engine: 11.7% of predicted frames decode exactly.

Next

Robust match evaluation

At least 100 pre-registered games per bot and policy, both seats and four maps, with Wilson intervals. This decides the CoacAI claim.

The causal value of belief

Matched belief versus no-belief PMPO over at least three seeds, with the same checkpoint rule and direct head-to-head, plus shuffled-z controls on every value and reward head.

Exact dynamics

Decoded-field grounding in the transition first, then multi-step self-rollout consistency, so imagination stays on the right side of the board’s discrete boundaries.
